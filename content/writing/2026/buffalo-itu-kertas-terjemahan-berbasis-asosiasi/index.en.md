My wife told me a story that left me unsure who to laugh at.

So my mother-in-law teaches English tutoring classes. One day, one of her students was practicing some exercises. The conversation went something like this (in Javanese, roughly translated):

> Student A: "Hey, what does 'buffalo' mean?"
>
> Student B: "Paper."
>
> Student A: "Be serious!"
>
> Student B: "I am! Buffalo paper. So it means paper."

I went quiet for a moment, then realized _the logic actually checks out_. That was what puzzled me.

## The Brain's Cache Is Too Powerful

If you've ever learned a foreign language, you may know the feeling: the brain doesn't store words like a dictionary. It stores them more like a cluttered folder.

The student hears the word _"buffalo"_. The brain immediately runs a search. The first result isn't an image of a large furry animal roaming the North American prairies. It's... **buffalo paper**. That's what he'd encountered most, what was closest, and what seemed most _relevant_ to his brain.

Linguists and language researchers call this _**association-based translation**_: people translate words using the memory most strongly associated with them, rather than a dictionary definition.[^1] That memory isn't always correct, but it can still _feel_ correct.

## This Isn't Just About Kids

Before you smile and say, _"well, of course, he's just a child"_, hold on.

Here are a few examples from everyday life.

**First example.** Have you heard someone think _"deadline"_ literally means "already dead"? For someone who's never worked in an environment where the term is used, the interpretation makes sense. _Dead_ means dead. _Line_ means line. So _deadline_: the death line? By association, reasonable. In a work context, not so much.

**Second example.** Some people new to the workforce understand _"overtime"_ as "extra time for rest." _Over_ = extra. _Time_ = time. So extra time? _Yes, extra time to keep working._ But they might picture the opposite haha.

**Third example, and this is the most ironic one.** Me. I once thought _"hardware"_ was a brand of stationery. A shop near my house called _"Toko Hardware"_ sold hard goods. I was still a bit confused about this well into high school. _Feel free to laugh._[^2]

## Why Our Brains Do This

This isn't an evolutionary bug. It's a feature.

The human brain prioritizes efficiency over accuracy. When you hear or read a word, it doesn't open an internal dictionary for one official definition. It asks its own _cache_: _"where have I encountered this word before? In what context? With whom?"_ The first answer that comes back is the one it uses.[^3]

The student who answered _"paper"_ wasn't stupid. His brain was working as it should; he just hadn't encountered enough varied uses of the word. He hadn't heard _"buffalo"_ often enough outside the context of paper, so buffalo paper was the only association he had.

And if you think about it further: **how many adults are doing the exact same thing, without realizing it?**

Plenty of assumptions we hold firmly turn out to be nothing more than the first association that ever entered our heads. Not truth. Just old _cache_ that was never updated.

## What the "Paper" Answer Can Teach Us

That answer from a tutoring session got me thinking about a few things.

First: **context determines meaning**. A word can have several meanings; the surrounding sentence helps us work out which one is intended.

Second: **the first association isn't necessarily the right one**. Our brains often serve up the _familiar_ answer rather than the _correct_ one. That can happen with language and with other decisions. _Though that's probably too heavy a topic for a post that started with a buffalo story._

Third: **if you're teaching, don't rush to make a student feel foolish**. The _"be serious!"_ response in the story was understandable, but the student's logic made sense in his world. We can correct him while respecting that logic: _"Yes, there is buffalo paper, but in English, buffalo is the animal — the big one, like a cow but wilder."_ That way, the student doesn't feel stupid.

My wife told me the story while holding back laughter. I laughed along too.

After laughing along, I found myself wondering: _how many other things have I "translated" based on a first association, then never questioned again?_

Probably a lot. I don't know how many.

[^1]: The more common term in the literature is _lexical association_ or _associative meaning_. But "association-based translation" felt like the most accurate way to describe what happens when someone translates a word based on contextual memory rather than its core meaning.

[^2]: Still haven't fully moved on from this fact.

[^3]: This relates to the concept of _priming_ in cognitive psychology, where exposure to one stimulus influences the response to the next. A brain that has been "primed" with buffalo paper will be faster to associate _buffalo_ with paper than with the animal.
