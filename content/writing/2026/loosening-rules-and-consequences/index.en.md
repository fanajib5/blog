In the middle of April, I typed out a fairly long message in a WhatsApp group for the students I teach as a lecturer. It was about [GitHub Education](https://education.github.com/pack), a GitHub program that gives free access to a collection of developer tools for enrolled students and educators.

Among those tools: GitHub Copilot. An AI assistant well-known in developer circles as a useful coding partner, with autocomplete, code review, and even writing chunks of code on its own.

I ended the message with a line that, thinking about it now, pretty accurately describes how I see my role as an educator:

> _I hope you all can be trustworthy with this. Because I'm already treating you as adults. If it turns out otherwise, well, that's no longer my responsibility. 😊_

I signed it off as "greetings from your fellow Gen-Z faculty, first generation," and laughed a little typing that hahaha.

## What I Did (and Why)

I recommended students register their campus email for GitHub Education. The process is straightforward: sign up, verify active student status, and they get access to various services for free, including GitHub Copilot with 300 prompt credits per month and access to a range of AI models inside it.

One note I made clear: Copilot is not a tool to outsource your assignments to AI wholesale. It's a learning partner, so you don't have to go through what I did, which was marathoning StackOverflow threads and YouTube tutorials from Indonesian creators, Western creators, and every Indian tutorial channel I could find hahaha.

I gave them a reminder, but no written ban, formal consequences, or monitoring system. I shared the access and left the decision about how to use it to each student. That's what I mean by loosening the rules.

## I Used to Be a Student Too

I can tell what some students might do with this access, because I've been in their position.

Back in my master's program, my thesis supervisor gave me remarkable freedom. Not much was required, not much was restricted. The essence was: _you set the direction, I guide you_[^1].

I used that freedom to put off my thesis. Repeatedly, for months. Looking back, my excuses were pretty weak hehe~

It wasn't a matter of being unable. It's just that once the constraints loosen, the temptation to use that space for other things gets very real. Webtoons read cover to cover. Games played until late at night. The thesis? Later, later...

So when I gave students Copilot access with just a reminder, I knew some of them would use it as a shortcut.

## So Why Do It Anyway?

Because not giving access doesn't stop students from finding these tools themselves.

Curious students will find them with or without their instructors. The difference, to me, is whether they find them knowing a bit about the limits or knowing nothing at all.

One line in my message put my concern plainly:

> _We won't be fully replaced by AI, at least not yet. But we might be replaced by someone who knows how to get the most out of AI in their work and life._

I wasn't trying to scare anyone. I've seen it in the field: two people with equal technical ability, but one is better at using AI as a tool. Their speed and output quality differ a lot.

I figured giving students an early start on working alongside AI is more useful than blocking access out of worry. That worry can't be fully prevented anyway.

## Loosening and Its Consequences

Loosening the rules has a price, of course.

When I said _"I'm treating you as adults"_, I wasn't washing my hands of it. I deliberately left the decision about using Copilot to each student. Trusting them isn't negligence. I consider them old enough to take responsibility for their choices.

Students who use Copilot as a learning buddy, to poke at concepts or understand things faster, will get real benefits. They'll have a head start when they enter the workforce.

Students who use it as a shortcut, finishing assignments without understanding them or handing everything to AI without a second read, will face the consequences too. Maybe not right away. It could show up in a job interview, in real-world problem solving, or when they have to modify code they don't know the origin of.

I can't protect them from that. It's not that I don't care, it's just that facing the results of your own choices is part of learning.

## A Teacher Who Isn't That Old Yet

Calling myself "Gen-Z faculty, first generation" felt accurate and a little absurd hahaha.

The gap between me the student and me the instructor isn't that wide. I still remember sitting on the other side: sleep-deprived, half-understanding things, and occasionally looking for shortcuts.

Maybe that thesis procrastination is why I picked trust over prohibition. I know firsthand how freedom can turn into months of delay. I also know a ban won't automatically stop students from finding AI tools. At least I want them to understand the tools they use before facing the consequences.

I gave them this leeway knowingly, even though how it turns out isn't entirely up to me.

I hope they use it well. 🙏
